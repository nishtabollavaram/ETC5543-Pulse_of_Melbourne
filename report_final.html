Pulse of Melbourne

Pedestrian rhythms, recovery and unusual activity.

Authors

Richa Anghan

Zhuo Zhang

Nishta Bollavaram

Abstract

Melbourne’s pedestrian activity reflects the city’s daily routines, spatial differences and responses to major disruptions. This project investigates how pedestrian movement varies across time and location, how these patterns have evolved, and which circumstances are associated with unusually high or low activity.

Using approximately 7.56 million hourly observations from the City of Melbourne’s pedestrian counting system, covering May 2009 to October 2026, we examined recurring daily, weekly, monthly and seasonal rhythms, long-term changes, geographical differences and exceptional activity. The analysis combined descriptive visualisation, consistent-sensor comparisons, K-means clustering and comparisons against expected pedestrian activity.

Results revealed recurring temporal patterns alongside substantial changes over time. Across 27 consistently monitored locations, typical daily pedestrian activity was 28.5% lower during 2023–2025 than during 2017–2019, although the overall hourly pattern remained broadly similar. K-means clustering of 44 reliably observed locations identified two activity profiles: one characterised by pronounced weekday peaks and another showing broader daytime activity and relatively stronger weekend patterns. The analysis also identified unusual pedestrian surges, including a median uplift of 51.7% on 22 February 2014, which was subsequently investigated alongside permitted city events.

These findings demonstrate that Melbourne’s pedestrian activity varies not only in volume but also in timing and location. They highlight the importance of accounting for sensor coverage, data quality and location-specific patterns when interpreting long-term changes and unusual activity. The results provide useful insights for understanding urban movement and informing transport, public-space and event planning.

1 Introduction

1.1 Understanding Melbourne’s Pedestrian Pulse

On 22 February 2014, pedestrian activity near Flinders Street and Elizabeth Street showed an unusual pattern as seen in Figure 1. Compared with other Saturdays in February, pedestrian counts remained noticeably higher, particularly into the evening. What would normally have been a relatively quiet period instead recorded sustained activity. This observation raised a broader question: was this an isolated occurrence at one location, or part of a larger change in how people were moving through Melbourne?

Melbourne’s streets serve different purposes throughout the day. Morning activity often reflects commuting, while shopping, leisure and other activities contribute to movement during the afternoon, evenings and weekends. These patterns can also vary across locations, seasons and years. Major disruptions, public events and weather conditions may further influence how frequently and when people move through the city.

Understanding these variations is important for urban planning and decision-making. Information about pedestrian activity can help identify busy periods and locations, support transport and infrastructure planning, and inform how public spaces are managed during major events. However, identifying meaningful changes requires more than simply comparing pedestrian counts. Activity recorded at a particular location may vary because of changes in travel behaviour, but also because of differences in sensor coverage, installation dates or data availability.

The City of Melbourne’s pedestrian counting system provides an opportunity to investigate these patterns using observations collected over an extended period. By examining both the timing and geographical distribution of pedestrian activity, this project explores how Melbourne’s movement follows familiar rhythms, how these rhythms have evolved, and when they depart from their usual patterns.

1.2 Research Aim and Objectives

The primary aim of this project is to investigate how pedestrian activity across Melbourne varies over time and space, how it has changed over the years, and which circumstances are associated with unusually high or low activity.

In line with the original project brief, the analysis addresses four related questions:

  1. What are Melbourne’s typical pedestrian rhythms? We investigate hourly, daily, weekly, monthly and seasonal variations to identify recurring patterns in pedestrian activity.

  2. How has pedestrian activity changed over time? We examine long-term trends, the disruption associated with COVID-19, and differences between pre-pandemic and more recent periods.

  3. How does pedestrian activity vary across locations? We investigate differences in pedestrian volumes and whether locations exhibit similar patterns of activity throughout weekdays and weekends.

  4. When does pedestrian activity depart from its usual patterns? We identify unusually busy and quiet days and examine their associations with major events, rainfall and public holidays.

Together, these questions allow the project to move beyond identifying where and when pedestrian counts are highest. They provide a framework for understanding how the city’s movement patterns differ, how they have evolved, and what may be associated with substantial changes.

2 Data Sources and Preparation

2.1 Data Sources

This project uses the City of Melbourne’s Pedestrian Counting System as its primary dataset, supplemented by sensor-location records, event permits, historical weather observations and Victorian public-holiday information. Together, these sources support the investigation of Melbourne’s pedestrian activity across time and space, including changes associated with major disruptions and unusual activity.

The pedestrian data consist of hourly observations obtained through historical CSV files and the City of Melbourne’s open data API (City of Melbourne, n.d.-c). Additional records covering 2022–2025 were obtained directly from the City of Melbourne through email correspondence to address gaps in publicly accessible records. Following integration and cleaning, the consolidated dataset contained 7,566,998 hourly observations, covering May 2009 to October 2026.

Supplementary datasets were used to provide geographical and contextual information. The City of Melbourne’s sensor-location dataset provided coordinates and installation information for spatial analysis (City of Melbourne, n.d.-b). Event permit records from the City of Melbourne were used to investigate whether unusual pedestrian activity coincided with permitted events(City of Melbourne, n.d.-a). Historical weather data from Open-Meteo provided rainfall and other weather observations (Open-Meteo, n.d.), while Victorian public-holiday information published by Business Victoria was used to identify public holidays (Business Victoria, n.d.).

Because the pedestrian sensor network expanded over time and individual sensors did not always report continuously, coverage varied across locations and years. These differences were considered during data preparation and when selecting observations for subsequent analyses.

2.2 Data Integration and Preparation

The datasets used in this project differed in their structure, coverage and level of detail. Before conducting the analysis, the pedestrian records were standardised and combined to create a consistent hourly dataset. Particular attention was given to differences between historical and recent records, repeated hour labels, overlapping observations and changes in sensor identification.

2.2.1 Standardising the Pedestrian Records

The historical pedestrian records and more recent datasets were initially stored in different formats. To allow them to be combined, the main variables were standardised to a common structure containing the sensor identifier (location_id), observation date (sensing_date), hour of the day (hourday) and pedestrian count (pedestrian_count).

Dates were converted to a consistent format, and sensor identifiers were used to link records across periods. This was particularly important because some sensor descriptions changed over time, meaning that the same monitoring location could appear under different names.

The combination of sensor identifier, date and hour was subsequently used as the observation key. This provided a consistent basis for identifying overlapping records and checking for duplicates.

2.2.2 Identifying and Correcting Data Inconsistencies

During data preparation, inconsistencies were identified in the historical hourly records. In particular, some sensor-day groups contained 24 observations but only one distinct hour label, preventing the observations from being directly assigned to their correct hours.

A total of 510 sensor-day groups exhibited this issue, representing 12,240 records. For these groups, hourly labels were reconstructed from 0 to 23 using the order of the original record identifiers. The affected observations were flagged during processing.

This correction relied on the assumption that record identifiers preserved the chronological order of observations. Because the original hour labels could not independently confirm this ordering, the reconstructed timestamps remain a source of uncertainty for analyses involving these records.

Overlapping observations from different data sources were examined to identify inconsistencies before combining the records. The comparison between the historical dataset and the City-supplied 2022 snapshot identified 464,481 matching sensor-date-hour records, of which 10,298 (approximately 2.2%) contained different pedestrian counts.

In contrast, the overlapping API and snapshot records compared for 2024 and 2025 showed no differences in pedestrian counts. These findings indicated that overlapping sources were not always interchangeable, particularly for 2022. To maintain a consistent selection of records, the City-supplied snapshots were used for 2022–2025, while the historical and API datasets supplied observations outside that period.

2.2.4 Dataset Description

After cleaning and combining the historical records, City-supplied snapshots and API data, the final dataset (pedestrian_full) contains approximately 7.56 million hourly observations from May 2009 to October 2026.

Each row represents the pedestrian count recorded at a particular sensor on a specific date and hour. The main variables identify the sensor location, observation date, hour and number of pedestrians counted. Additional variables were created to identify the year, month, day of the week, weekend status and season, allowing pedestrian activity to be examined across different time periods.

2.3 Preparing Supporting Datasets

Additional datasets were prepared to support specific parts of the investigation. Sensor-location records were used to associate pedestrian observations with geographical coordinates and installation information, while event permit data provided dates and contextual information for examining unusual activity.

Historical daily weather observations were also prepared, including maximum and minimum temperature, maximum wind speed and rainfall. These records were converted to a common date format and restricted to the study period. Victorian public-holiday records were filtered to retain official public holidays, with duplicate dates removed.

These supporting datasets were used to provide context for pedestrian activity, particularly when examining unusual changes in daily counts. The methods used to calculate typical pedestrian activity, identify anomalies and examine their relationships with weather conditions and public holidays are explained in the Methodology section.

The event permit records required additional preparation because event categories were not consistently labelled and permit durations varied considerably. Event categories were standardised, and permits were classified according to their suitability for date-based matching.

3 Analytical Methodology

The basic unit is the sensor-hour. For daily measures, counts were summed across available hours for each sensor and date, excluding days with fewer than 20 observed hours; clustering used a 23-hour threshold. Citywide statements use the median across sensors rather than the sum.

Two normalised measures make comparison across years possible. The hourly activity index divides the average count at each hour by the year’s overall mean and multiplies by 100; the weekly index applies the same logic to days of the week. Because each year is normalised against its own mean, the index describes the shape of the rhythm rather than its absolute level. Absolute change is measured separately using median daily counts.

Long-term change uses a balanced panel of 27 sensors that recorded at least 300 days in every year from 2017 to 2019 and 2023 to 2025. This reduces the influence of changes in sensor coverage when comparing pedestrian activity across periods. For each sensor-day, counts were summed across hours; median daily counts were calculated for the two periods, and percentage change derived from those medians. The COVID-19 analysis instead indexes each sensor-month against its own same-month mean for 2015–2019, with the citywide index taken as the median across sensors with at least 20 observed days.

Unusual days are defined against a local calendar expectation. For each sensor, day of week and date, expected activity is the median daily count on the same weekday in the four weeks before and after the date, excluding the date itself. A significant citywide surge requires median uplift of at least +20% and at least 30% of sensors more than 20% above expectation; a dip uses the mirror thresholds, with at least ten sensors reporting.

Holiday enrichment compares the share of significant dips on public holidays with the share of all eligible days that are holidays. Weather comparisons exclude public holidays, and rainfall is examined as a 10 mm threshold and in six ordered groups.

K-means clustering uses a 48-dimensional profile of typical activity shares across weekday and weekend hours. Sensors with at least 80% daily coverage in 2023–2025 were retained (44 locations). Profiles were compared by Euclidean distance, and two-to-five-cluster solutions evaluated with mean silhouette width. The two-cluster solution performed best (0.442, versus 0.252–0.270) and is used descriptively.

4 Results and Findings

This section presents the main findings from the pedestrian counting data, beginning with Melbourne’s daily, weekly and seasonal patterns. It then examines how activity has changed over time, differences between locations, and unusual periods of pedestrian activity. Where relevant, comparisons account for changes in sensor coverage to avoid interpreting network expansion as a change in pedestrian movement.

4.1 Daily, Weekly and Seasonal Pedestrian Patterns

Pedestrian activity in Melbourne varies throughout the day, across the week and between different times of the year. To understand these patterns, hourly and weekly activity indices were used to compare the relative timing of pedestrian activity across years. Monthly and seasonal summaries were also calculated to examine broader patterns in pedestrian counts.

4.1.1 Daily Pedestrian Activity Over Time

The hourly activity profiles reveal a consistent daily pattern across the observed years. Figure 2 shows that pedestrian activity is generally lowest overnight, increases sharply during the morning, and remains elevated throughout the middle of the day and late afternoon. The profiles show noticeable peaks during typical commuting periods, particularly in the morning and late afternoon, although their relative intensity varies between years.

Despite differences in activity levels over time, the overall shape of the daily rhythm remains broadly recognisable. As the activity index is normalised against each year’s average, the graph highlights differences in the timing and relative intensity of pedestrian movement rather than absolute changes in pedestrian volumes. These changes in overall activity are examined separately in Section 4.2.

4.1.2 Weekly rhythm across the years

The weekly activity index shows clear differences in pedestrian movement across weekdays and weekends. Across the observed years in Figure 3, Friday generally records the highest relative activity, with a median index of 115.8, followed by Thursday (105.6) and Wednesday (103.6). Sunday records the lowest median index at 82.9.

However, the relative difference between Friday and Saturday has narrowed. In 2019, Friday recorded an index of 115.5 compared with 94.5 on Saturday, a difference of 21.0 index points. By 2025, Friday and Saturday recorded indices of 108.9 and 107.5, respectively, reducing the gap to just 1.4 points. Over the same period, Monday’s index decreased from 96.5 to 89.5, while Sunday’s increased from 78.7 to 87.9.

These findings suggest a shift in the distribution of pedestrian activity across the week, with Saturday becoming more comparable to Friday in relative terms.

4.1.3 Monthly Pedestrian Rhythm

Melbourne’s pedestrian activity shows a recurring pattern throughout the year. Hourly counts were aggregated into daily totals for each sensor, and average daily activity was then calculated for each calendar month using the available observations from 2009 to 2026.

The monthly results in Figure 4 reveal a recurring pattern in pedestrian activity, with relatively high average daily counts during March and December and lower activity during the winter months. March records the highest average daily count, while June records the lowest, followed by a gradual recovery towards the end of the year.

These variations may be associated with seasonal weather conditions, university schedules, retail activity and major public events. However, the descriptive averages do not independently establish the influence of these factors.

4.1.4 Seasonal pedestrian rhythm

To examine broader seasonal differences, pedestrian activity was grouped into summer, autumn, winter and spring. Average daily pedestrian counts were calculated for each season using the available sensor observations.

The seasonal results support the monthly findings, with summer and autumn showing relatively higher pedestrian activity and winter recording the lowest counts. Activity increases again during spring, suggesting that pedestrian movement follows a recurring seasonal pattern.(Figure 5)

These differences are relevant to transport planning, public-space management and businesses that experience changes in pedestrian demand throughout the year. However, the results should be interpreted with some caution because sensor coverage changed over time and the dataset includes partial years.

Overall, the monthly and seasonal patterns provide context for understanding longer-term changes in pedestrian activity, particularly the disruption and recovery associated with COVID-19.

4.2 Long-term and Covid-19 changes

Melbourne’s pedestrian activity has undergone substantial changes over time, particularly during and after the COVID-19 pandemic. While the previous section examined recurring daily, weekly and seasonal patterns, this section investigates changes in the overall level of pedestrian activity and compares recent observations with pre-pandemic conditions.

A key consideration is the expansion of Melbourne’s pedestrian sensor network. Comparing total pedestrian counts across different years may be misleading because the number and composition of reporting locations have changed over time.

To improve comparability, the analysis identifies 27 sensor locations that recorded observations on at least 300 days in each of the six selected years: 2017–2019 and 2023–2025. Median daily activity across these consistently monitored locations is then compared between the two periods.

This approach provides evidence of changes at selected monitoring locations while reducing the influence of sensor network expansion. A separate comparison of hourly activity patterns is also used to examine whether the timing of pedestrian movement has changed alongside overall volumes.

4.2.1 Long term pedestrian trends

Figure 6 shows substantial variation in pedestrian activity between 2009 and 2026. A particularly pronounced decline is visible during the COVID-19 period, followed by a recovery in subsequent years. Nevertheless, activity in the more recent period does not consistently reach the higher levels observed before the pandemic.

Consequently, this series provides a broad historical overview, while the comparison of 27 consistently monitored locations in the following section offers a more reliable assessment of changes between the selected pre-pandemic and recent periods.

4.2.2 Pedestrian Activity Before and After Covid-19

Figure 7 shows a substantial reduction in typical daily pedestrian activity across the 27 consistently monitored locations. Median daily counts declined from approximately 10,091 during 2017–2019 to 7,211 during 2023–2025, representing a decrease of 28.5%.

Because the same locations were included in both periods, the comparison reduces the influence of sensor network expansion and provides a more consistent measure of long-term change. The findings indicate that pedestrian activity at these selected locations remained below its pre-pandemic level during 2023–2025.

4.2.3 Changes in Hourly Pedestrian Activity

Figure 8 shows that the general shape of Melbourne’s pedestrian activity remained recognisable between the two periods. Both 2017–2019 and 2023–2025 exhibit relatively low activity overnight, followed by increasing pedestrian movement during the morning and higher counts throughout the daytime.

However, the 2023–2025 profile remains below the pre-pandemic profile across much of the day, with particularly noticeable differences around the main daytime peaks. This suggests that although the timing of pedestrian movement remained broadly similar, its intensity declined at the selected monitoring locations.

The observed differences may be associated with changes in commuting, workplace attendance and city-centre activity.

4.3 Outlier days, events and public holidays

Pedestrian activity occasionally departs substantially from its usual daily and seasonal patterns. While some dates record unusually high pedestrian volumes, others experience widespread declines. Examining these deviations helps identify periods when movement across Melbourne’s monitored locations differs from typical conditions.

This section investigates two types of unusual activity. First, pedestrian surges are identified and compared with permitted events recorded by the City of Melbourne. Second, unusually quiet days are examined alongside Victorian public holidays and weather conditions, particularly rainfall.

4.3.1 Unusual Pedestrian Surges: associations with permitted events

To identify unusually busy pedestrian days, daily counts were compared with expected activity at each sensor. Only sensor-days containing at least 20 distinct recorded hours were retained to reduce the influence of incomplete observations.

Expected activity was estimated using the median daily count from the four preceding and four following available observations on the same day of the week, excluding the date being evaluated. This approach accounts for typical differences between weekdays and weekends. However, when observations are missing, the comparison window may extend beyond four calendar weeks.

For each sensor-day, pedestrian uplift was calculated as the percentage difference between the observed and expected daily count. For example, an observed count of 1,500 pedestrians compared with an expected count of 1,000 represents an uplift of 50%. The median percentage uplift across reporting sensors was then used to summarise activity for each date.

A date was classified as shown in Figure 9, as a potential citywide pedestrian surge when at least 10 sensors reported valid comparisons, the median uplift reached at least 20%, and at least 30% of reporting sensors recorded an uplift of 20% or more. Consecutive qualifying dates were grouped into episodes to avoid treating related increases on adjacent dates as separate occurrences.

The resulting episodes were compared with City of Melbourne event-permit records covering 2014–2018. Short-duration permits were matched using overlapping event and episode dates, and a match was retained when the episode’s peak-uplift date fell within the permit period.

4.3.2 Unusual pedestrian dips: associations with public holidays and rainfall

Apart from major events, pedestrian activity can also fall substantially below its usual level. This section examines whether unusually quiet days are associated with Victorian public holidays and weather conditions, particularly rainfall.

As in the event analysis, daily pedestrian activity was compared with nearby observations from the same sensor and day of the week. A significant pedestrian dip was identified when at least 10 sensors were reporting, the median change was −20% or lower, and at least 30% of reporting sensors recorded a decrease of 20% or more. These criteria help identify declines affecting several monitored locations rather than isolated changes at individual sensors.

Christmas Day was among the dates associated with particularly large declines. This may reflect changes in everyday routines, including commuting, shopping and other activities that usually contribute to pedestrian movement in the city.

After excluding public holidays, the median rainfall was 11.3 mm on significant dip days, compared with 0.2 mm on other days. Overall, public holidays and rainfall both appear to be associated with unusually low pedestrian activity in Melbourne. Public holidays may change regular commuting and shopping patterns, while heavier rainfall may discourage outdoor movement.

4.4 Spatial differences in pedestrian activity

Pedestrian activity is not distributed evenly across Melbourne. Some locations experience consistently high pedestrian volumes, while others have different patterns depending on their surroundings and how people use the area.

An important consideration is the growth of the pedestrian sensor network over time. As more sensors were installed, the number of monitored locations increased, making direct comparisons of total counts across years less reliable. The following analysis therefore examines individual locations and uses coverage requirements to improve comparability.

Figure 12 illustrates the expansion of the sensor network through new installations. However, installation counts do not necessarily represent the number of sensors actively reporting pedestrian observations in each year, as individual sensors may experience interruptions or changes in operation.

Figure 14 show that pedestrian activity is concentrated around Melbourne’s major retail, transport and commercial areas. Flinders La–Swanston St (West) recorded the highest typical daily count at approximately 40,000 pedestrians, followed by Town Hall (West) at approximately 36,000. Other busy monitored locations include Southbank Promenade, Elizabeth Street and the QV precinct.

Figure 13 illustrates the geographic distribution of pedestrian activity, while the top-10 ranking identifies the busiest monitored locations in 2025. These findings highlight the importance of Melbourne’s central pedestrian corridors.

However, the results represent pedestrian counts at individual sensor locations rather than total pedestrian activity across entire streets or precincts. Locations without sufficient observations during the selected period were excluded.

Comparing recent activity with pre-COVID conditions reveals further differences between Melbourne’s monitored locations. For this analysis, average daily pedestrian counts were compared with the corresponding calendar-month baseline from 2015–2019. The recent recovery index was calculated using available observations from 2024–2026.

Figure 15 shows that the extent of recovery varies between locations. Some sensors record activity closer to or above their earlier levels, while others remain substantially below their pre-pandemic baseline. This suggests that Melbourne’s recovery has not been uniform across the city.

The comparison uses same-month baselines to account for recurring seasonal differences. However, it remains a descriptive analysis, and changes in surrounding land use, transport patterns or sensor conditions may also affect the results. The 2026 observations are incomplete, so the recent period should not be interpreted as three full calendar years.

4.4.1 Checking the Flinders Underpass Result

One location requiring closer examination was Flinders Underpass – Myki Barriers, where the recovery analysis(Figure 15) indicated a particularly large decline of approximately 72.6% compared with its pre-COVID baseline.

The sensor metadata records an upgrade in September 2021, raising the question of whether changes to the monitoring equipment could have affected the comparison. To investigate this, the monthly pedestrian counts were examined alongside the recorded upgrade date. A further sensitivity check compared the overall spatial results with and without this location.

4.4.2 K-means Clustering for pedestrian patterns

To understand how pedestrian activity differs between locations, K-means clustering was applied to 44 sensor locations with sufficient data coverage during 2023–2025. Rather than comparing locations based only on their total pedestrian counts, the analysis focused on how activity was distributed throughout the day.

For each location, typical hourly pedestrian activity was calculated separately for weekdays and weekends. These values were converted into proportions of daily activity, producing 48 features per sensor: 24 hourly proportions for weekdays and 24 for weekends. This allowed locations to be grouped according to the shape of their pedestrian activity patterns rather than their overall pedestrian volumes.

The number of clusters was assessed using the elbow method and silhouette scores. Based on these results, two clusters were selected, with an average silhouette score of approximately 0.442. The final K-means model grouped 9 sensor locations into Cluster 1 and 35 into Cluster 2.

Figure 17 show that the two clusters follow different daily rhythms. One group has more concentrated activity during typical weekday commuting hours, with noticeable morning and late-afternoon peaks. The other group shows a broader distribution of activity throughout the day, with relatively stronger weekend activity. These differences suggest that pedestrian movement varies not only in volume but also in when locations are most active.

Figure 18 shows where these two activity patterns occur across Melbourne. Viewing the geographic locations alongside their hourly profiles helps identify areas with similar movement patterns, even when they are not located next to one another.

Overall, the clustering analysis identifies two distinct patterns of pedestrian activity among the 44 selected locations. Together with the busiest-location rankings and spatial recovery analysis, it shows that Melbourne’s pedestrian network varies in both the intensity and timing of movement.

5 Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the analysis is observational. It identifies associations between pedestrian activity and holidays, events and weather, but cannot establish causal effects. Public holidays and events are not randomly assigned, weather is correlated with season, and unobserved factors such as transport disruptions, school holidays and construction could confound the results.

Second, the sensor network is not a random sample of the city. It is concentrated in the CBD and surrounding activity centres, and its composition has changed over time. The 27-sensor balanced panel reduces this problem for the long-term comparison, but may not represent the whole municipality. The 44-sensor clustering sample is also restricted to high-coverage locations.

Third, the historical data required repair. The 2022 reconciliation identified 10,298 records with differing pedestrian counts, concentrated at location 6. In addition, 12,240 records from September 2010 required reconstruction of their hour labels using record order. Although the repaired records passed subsequent consistency checks, the reconstruction introduces some uncertainty into the hourly patterns.

Fourth, the event analysis is limited by permit coverage. The permit dataset covers 2014–2018, so event matching could only be conducted for dates within this period. Surges outside 2014–2018 were identified statistically but could not be matched using this permit dataset. A single date may also host multiple events, making a surge difficult to attribute to one cause.

Fifth, the definition of a “significant” day uses thresholds—20% deviation and 30% of sensors—that are convenient but ultimately arbitrary; different thresholds would produce different counts of outlier days. The results therefore identify days meeting the selected thresholds rather than every possible unusual activity day.

Sixth, the weather analysis uses daily aggregates from a single series. Rainfall can be localised, and a citywide total may not reflect conditions at a particular sensor. Temperature results are bivariate and should not be read causally. Finally, clustering is descriptive and cross-sectional; it shows that two profiles dominate 2023–2025, not that Melbourne has exactly two types of place or that locations cannot change type.

6 Conclusion

Melbourne’s pedestrian pulse is neither constant nor fully restored to its pre-pandemic pattern. It follows a recognisable rhythm across different hours, weekdays and seasons, with unusual increases and declines occurring around public holidays, events and different weather conditions. The long-term comparison identified a 28.5% reduction in median daily pedestrian activity across consistently monitored locations between 2017–2019 and 2023–2025. However, this change is not uniform across locations or times of day, highlighting how the nature of pedestrian movement has evolved.

The findings suggest that understanding Melbourne’s pedestrian activity requires more than measuring overall foot traffic. Changes in commuting patterns, differences between precincts and the effects of external conditions all contribute to the city’s movement. These insights can support more informed decisions about transport, public spaces, business operations and urban planning.

Ultimately, the most useful question is not simply whether Melbourne has recovered, but how its pedestrian patterns have changed and what those changes mean for the future. A city’s pulse is not a single number but a pattern of times and places, and the better that pattern is understood, the better the city can respond.
